## Find the person who books the rides

Nobody at a company searches "corporate car service" and signs a contract. Someone books one ride for a visiting executive, it goes well, and they book the next one. The person who books is rarely the CEO. It is the executive assistant, the office manager, the travel coordinator, or at a law firm the legal administrator.

Make a list of fifty companies in your market that fly people in: law firms, hospitals, the regional offices of national companies, private equity and consulting shops, the universities. For each one, find the name of the person in that role. LinkedIn gives it to you in a minute, and the leads tool gives you the name, the title and a verified email for each business on the list.

## Lead with the problem they have

The assistant booking rides has one fear: the car doesn't show. Every pitch should answer that fear before it mentions your fleet. A chauffeur who tracks the flight, texts when they are on the way and waits at the right door is worth more to that person than a newer vehicle.

So the first email is short, and it is about them:

- One line on who you are and how long you have driven the city.
- One line on what you do for companies like theirs: airport pickups that follow the flight, one invoice a month, a booking link their team can use.
- One ask: a single ride, at your corporate rate, so they can see how it goes.

No brochure, no attachment, no "I hope this email finds you well". A hundred and twenty words at most, and a subject line that names their company.

## Make the first ride impossible to fault

The first booking from a new account is an audition. Confirm it in writing the same day. Send the chauffeur's name and the vehicle the night before. Track the flight and move the pickup when it lands early. Text the passenger when the car is in place. Send the receipt within the hour.

Then, a day later, send the booker one line: "Hope Mr. Alvarez's ride went smoothly. Here is the link your team can use for the next one." That link is the account.

<Quote>The account is won on the second ride, not the first. The first proves you can do it; the second proves they don't have to think about it.</Quote>

## Give them a reason to stay

Once a company has booked three rides, offer the account properly: a corporate rate, monthly invoicing, a portal where their people book under the company's name, and a report at the end of the month showing every ride. This is the part most operators skip, and it is why their corporate work stays at one ride a month.

Offer it in person if you can. A twenty-minute visit to the office with coffee does more than ten emails, because the assistant now has a face to call when the executive's plans change at 9pm.

## Keep the pipeline moving

The operators who win accounts treat outreach like dispatch: a list, a status for each name, and a next action with a date. Five new asks a week, a follow-up on everyone who didn't answer after six days, and a note on every conversation. In three months that is sixty companies reached, and at the rates we see, four to six of them become accounts.

## Conclusion

Corporate work comes from a short list, a short email about their problem, a first ride that can't be faulted, and an account offer made after the third booking. Do that for an hour a week and the accounts arrive, one assistant at a time.
