## 1. Choose the work you'll do

"Limo company" covers very different businesses, and each needs different vehicles, insurance and clients. Pick one to start:

- **Airport and corporate black car.** Sedans and SUVs, steady weekday work, clients who book again and again. The most predictable start.
- **Weddings, proms and events.** Stretch limos, sprinters and party buses, higher rates per booking, busy weekends and quiet weekdays.
- **Tours and intercity runs.** Longer trips at set prices, often seasonal.

Most operators start with one SUV or sedan in airport and corporate work, and add event vehicles once the weekdays are full.

## 2. Set up the company

Register the business, usually as an LLC, and get an EIN from the IRS. Open a business bank account and keep every dollar of the business in it from day one. Pick a name you can get the matching domain and social handles for, since riders will search for it.

## 3. Get the permits

Limo licensing depends on where you operate, and it's set by the state, the county, the city or all three. A few examples:

- **California:** a Transportation Charter Party (TCP) permit from the California Public Utilities Commission.
- **New York City:** a base license and vehicle and driver licenses from the Taxi and Limousine Commission.
- **Across state lines:** interstate operating authority from the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, unless you stay within a commercial zone, per the [FMCSA](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/safety/passenger-safety/licensing-and-insurance-requirements-hire-motor-carriers-passengers).

Airports usually need their own permit to pick up passengers, with their own fees and rules. Call your state's transportation or utilities regulator, your city and your airport's ground transportation office before buying anything; they'll tell you what applies.

## 4. Get insured

Insurance is the step that surprises new operators most. Expect $5,000 to $10,000 a year per sedan or SUV, and more for larger vehicles. For interstate work, the federal minimum is $1.5 million in liability for vehicles carrying 15 or fewer people, including the driver, and $5 million for 16 or more. Get quotes before you choose a vehicle, since the vehicle changes the premium. The full breakdown is in [what limo insurance costs per vehicle](/journal/limo-insurance-cost).

## 5. Get the vehicles

Buy, lease or finance, but match the vehicle to the work you chose in step one. A new stretch limousine can cost $90,000 to $250,000, according to [Insurify](https://insurify.com/business-insurance/commercial-auto/limo/), while a late-model SUV costs a fraction of that and does most airport and corporate work. Check that the vehicle meets your permit's age and condition rules before you sign.

## 6. Set your rates

Work out your rates from your costs: the vehicle payment, insurance, maintenance, driver pay and fuel, spread over the hours you'll actually be booked. Then check the result against what operators in your city charge. The method, with a worked example, is in [limo rates: what operators charge and how to set yours](/journal/limo-rates).

## 7. Set up booking, dispatch and payments

You need a way for riders to book and pay, and a way to run each ride. For one vehicle, that can start simple, but it gets hard fast once you have a second car or a second driver. Decide early whether you'll take bookings on your own website or through a marketplace that keeps a share of each fare. Our guides to [what dispatch software has to do](/journal/limo-dispatch-software) and [limo booking software compared](/journal/limo-booking-software-compared) cover the options.

## 8. Get found online

Set up your Google Business Profile and your website before the first ride, with the same name, address and phone on both. For many limo companies, most search traffic comes from the Google map listing, so reviews start mattering from the first ride. The [website checklist for year one](/journal/black-car-business-website-checklist) lists what the site needs, in order.

## 9. Win the first clients

Your first steady work will come from people who book rides for others: corporate travel managers, executive assistants, hotel concierges, wedding planners and event venues. Make a list of fifty in your area and start reaching out before launch. [How to get corporate clients](/journal/how-to-get-corporate-clients-limo-company) walks through the outreach.

## What goes in a limo business plan

A lender or investor will want a plan, and writing one forces the decisions above. Keep it short and specific:

- **The service.** The work you'll do, the vehicles and the area you'll cover.
- **The market.** Who books rides in your area: the airports, the corporate offices, the hotels and venues, and the competitors already serving them.
- **Pricing.** Your rates, with the cost math behind them.
- **Getting clients.** Your website and Google plan, and the list of first accounts you'll approach.
- **Operations.** Permits, insurance, drivers, booking and dispatch, maintenance.
- **The numbers.** Startup costs, monthly fixed costs, the billable hours you need to break even and a first-year forecast.

## Conclusion

Choose the work, set up the company, get the permits and insurance, then pick vehicles that fit both. Price from your own costs, set up booking and payments, get found online and start on your first accounts before launch day.
