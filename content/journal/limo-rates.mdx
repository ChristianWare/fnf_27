## What goes on top of the hourly rate

The hourly rate is rarely the whole price. The usual additions:

- **Minimum hours.** Two to three hours is common for hourly bookings, with higher minimums on Friday and Saturday nights, prom season, holiday weekends and big event dates.
- **Gratuity.** Many operators add 15 to 20% automatically. Say so clearly on the quote, so riders don't tip twice and then feel overcharged.
- **Fuel surcharge.** A percentage that moves with fuel prices, so you don't have to reprice every month.
- **Tolls, parking and airport fees.** Passed through at cost.
- **Wait time and overtime.** Billed in set increments, often 15 minutes, once the booked time runs over.
- **Meet and greet.** A flat fee for the chauffeur to meet the rider inside the terminal with a sign.

Together, gratuity, fuel and wait time can add 15 to 25% to the base rate. Show the full price before the rider books; a total that grows at checkout is the fastest way to lose a booking.

## Airport transfers: flat rates, not hourly

Airport runs are usually priced as a flat rate from a pickup zone to the terminal, not by the hour. Set one price for each suburb or zip code you serve, for each vehicle type, then add the airport's fees and any meet-and-greet. Flat rates are easy for riders to compare and easy for you to publish, and a page for each airport with the rates on it is one of the [pages worth building](/journal/seo-for-limo-companies) for search.

## How to set your own rate

Copying a competitor's price tells you what the market will bear, not whether you'll make money. Work up from your own costs instead.

**1. Add up your fixed costs for a month.** Everything you pay whether or not the vehicle is booked: the vehicle payment, insurance, maintenance, cleaning, phone, software and website.

**2. Estimate your billable hours.** The hours a month the vehicle is actually booked, not the hours it's available. Be honest; for a new operator, 60 to 100 hours a month per vehicle is a reasonable starting assumption.

**3. Add the cost of each hour on the road.** The driver's pay and the fuel.

**4. Add your margin.** What's left after costs pays you, covers slow months and funds the next vehicle.

At $66 an hour in costs, a $110 hourly rate leaves about $44 an hour, a 40% margin, and sits near the bottom of the typical SUV range. If your costs come out higher than the local going rate, the fix is usually more billable hours, not a higher price: corporate accounts and airport work fill the weekday gaps that event work leaves. ## Publish your rates

A rider comparing three companies late at night is more likely to book the one that shows a price. Put your airport flat rates and hourly minimums on your website, and let riders get an exact quote and book without calling. If a booking platform takes a percentage of every fare, add that to your costs too; the [booking fee calculator](/tools/booking-fee-calculator) shows what it comes to in a year.

## Conclusion

Use the market ranges to see where you fit, then build your rate from your own costs so every booked hour makes money. Add minimums, gratuity and surcharges in writing, price airport runs as flat rates, and show riders the full price before they book.
